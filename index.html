I am a Postdoctoral Research Associate at the University of Cambridge, working in the Microelectronics Group at the Cavendish Laboratory. I am also a postdoctoral affiliate member of Trinity College, Cambridge. My current research focuses on superconducting spintronics, vortex dynamics and nonequilibrium superconductivity for emerging cryogenic and quantum technologies.

I received my PhD in Electrical Engineering from the Indian Institute of Technology Delhi (IIT Delhi), where my doctoral research focused on magnetoelectric spintronic devices and multifunctional lab-on-a-chip technologies for energy-efficient computing and biomedical applications.

Vortex dynamics in superconductors

My research investigates the dynamics of magnetic flux vortices in superconducting microdevices under applied magnetic fields and transport currents. I focus on vortex pinning, depinning, flux-flow regimes and vortex-flow instabilities. Through low-temperature electrical transport measurements, I aim to understand the mechanisms governing vortex motion and the onset of dissipation in superconducting structures. These investigations contribute towards the development of functional superconducting devices and emerging cryogenic technologies.

Microwave-driven nonequilibrium superconductivity

My research explores how high-frequency electromagnetic excitation modifies superconducting properties beyond equilibrium. I investigate microwave-induced changes in quasiparticle dynamics, critical currents and dissipative states using cryogenic experimental platforms. The objective is to understand the interplay between electromagnetic excitation and the superconducting state, enabling new approaches to controlling superconducting devices for cryogenic electronics and quantum technologies.

Superconducting spintronics

My research explores how superconductivity and magnetism interact in hybrid superconductor–ferromagnet heterostructures. I investigate spin-dependent transport and magnetisation dynamics using cryogenic electrical measurements and ferromagnetic resonance spectroscopy. A central aim is to understand how magnetic configurations influence superconducting transport and to explore the conditions for spin-polarised supercurrents. These studies seek to establish routes towards low-dissipation spintronic devices, energy-efficient cryogenic memory and logic, and hybrid quantum technologies.

Magnetoelectric spintronics & optical control

I explore the coupling between electric, optical, elastic and magnetic degrees of freedom in magnetoelectric heterostructures. My work investigates how photo-induced strain can modify magnetoresistance and magnetisation dynamics in ferromagnetic thin films integrated with ferroelectric substrates. Electrical transport measurements and ferromagnetic resonance spectroscopy provide complementary ways to probe these effects. The long-term aim is to develop low-energy methods of controlling magnetic states for nonvolatile memory, logic and beyond-CMOS devices.

Magnetoelectrics for biomedical applications

My research explores magnetoelectric heterostructures as functional platforms for manipulating and detecting magnetic particles in microfluidic environments. I investigate strain-controlled magnetisation and domain-wall motion in patterned magnetic microstructures, together with magnetoelectric sensing approaches. The objective is to enable controlled particle transport and quantitative detection on integrated devices. These approaches have potential applications in compact lab-on-a-chip systems, biomedical sensing and diagnostic technologies.
